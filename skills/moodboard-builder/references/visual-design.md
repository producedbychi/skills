# Visual design guidance

Use this guidance when composing or revising a rendered reference board. Let the project evidence shape the design. The renderer's layout options are tools for arranging that evidence, not a fixed visual theme.

## Lead with the references

Give images enough size to judge their composition, color, texture, and detail at presentation size. Image role and aspect ratio should determine the space each reference gets. A lead image may be much larger than support images; equal-priority references may share space. Use a small image only when it is genuinely secondary to the decision.

Arrange related evidence so the reviewer can see the proposed visual language together. A direction may need a lead scene, a material detail, and a smaller identity check on one page. An identity photo preserves a person's or product's appearance; it does not automatically deserve the same space as the scene that establishes light and framing. Use a separate identity sheet when many accuracy checks would crowd the direction. For an equal comparison, use equal visual weight. Choose the relationship first, then the layout.

When using the supplied renderer, choose `direction.layout` for the comparison. These layouts are conveniences, not the limit of what the skill can design:

- `hero` when each reference needs its own large study page.
- `lead-support` when the first card leads and the remaining cards explain or support it.
- `pair` when two references need a direct, equal comparison.
- `gallery` when several references have equal priority. Let the renderer continue the gallery across pages as needed.
- `mosaic` when a larger lead and smaller supporting references need to be seen together. Put the lead first. Keep support notes concise and check that their images remain large enough to judge.

Keep each image intact unless a detail crop makes a specific point. Group its observation and application with the image. Keep those notes short. For example, "Observe: side light reveals the ceramic ridges. Apply: light the supplied bottle to show its surface texture." Put full source, creator, URL, and rights details in a compact provenance section. Source thumbnails help the reviewer match records to images. Label unknown information as unknown.

Make the role legible beside the image when it affects interpretation, such as "light reference" or "identity only." If the source depicts a different flavor, product, person, or fictional object, say exactly what to borrow and what to replace. Keep important identity details visible in the accuracy reference, even when an inspiration crop emphasizes another quality.

## Derive a project-specific visual language

Read the supplied brief, approved brand material, and selected references as visual evidence. Look for useful relationships in their palette, light, material, contrast, framing, typography, and graphic treatment. Apply the relevant relationships to the board's page rhythm and image handling.

Use client-approved type, color, and product details as facts. Mark a new palette, type treatment, logo, or other identity change as proposed. Keep the board's information hierarchy steady while allowing its compositions and visual tone to follow the chosen direction. Do not add a decorative theme that has no support in the brief or references.

Directions should differ in what the client would approve, such as the framing, lighting, material, image sequence, or graphic treatment. A common deck grid can help the client compare them. The distinction must appear in the selected imagery and project choices, not only a new title. Tie the contrast to a brief goal and state its material tradeoff. The board's design presents the references; it does not turn every borrowed reference into a new client identity.

Match the lead view to the decision's scale and emphasis. When the proposed framing changes the reference's dominant composition, choose a closer source or make a deliberate detail crop, with the intact source accessible. If the available imagery supports only the light or material, label that narrower role and identify the framing evidence still needed. Keep an unresolved visual choice explicit rather than asking the client to approve it from a contradictory lead image.

Show the direction's application and meaningful tradeoff where the client is viewing the imagery. A short choice note can be enough. Add a visual comparison only when it makes the decision easier; a separate text-only summary is not mandatory. Color swatches, type samples, or detail studies earn a place when they clarify a proposed decision, not as decoration or a required moodboard checklist.

## Plan spacing before placing content

Reserve each panel's inner padding before positioning its text and images. Size the panel for the complete caption stack, including the last label and the bottom inset. Intentional image bleed can coexist with padded captions; it does not set the text inset.

For a standard widescreen slide, start with about 0.20 inches of horizontal text padding and 0.16 to 0.25 inches above and below the text. Scale those starting values with the page and type size. Use smaller gaps within a related caption stack and larger gaps between independent groups. Keep the spacing consistent across equivalent cards. These are starting dimensions, not a required card design.

Measure the complete text block, including wrapped lines and descenders. Place that block inside the intended inner area. If it does not fit, enlarge the container, reflow the composition, or shorten redundant copy. Keep the chosen readable type size. A label that technically fits but sits against the panel edge still needs correction.

Spacing passes when each caption and callout has visible clearance above and below its rendered text, equivalent cards use consistent insets, and neighboring groups remain distinct. Check the bottom line as carefully as the first line. Text-box bounds provide a preflight check; the rendered ink determines the final result.

## Check the rendered pages

Review every page at the size the client will use. Images should carry the direction, headings should state the choice, and annotations should help compare the evidence. Check detail visibility and intentional crops, text legibility, consistent alignment, and the absence of clipping or overlap. Rework pages where the image field is too small to assess, repeated labels compete with the reference, or unused space weakens the composition. Check continuation pages as well as the opening pages. Redistribute short source lists or choose another composition when automatic pagination leaves an unnecessary near-empty slide. Vary compositions when the content calls for them. Repeat a layout when equal comparison needs it.

Check the board without its explanatory paragraphs. Can the imagery still communicate the important difference between directions? Then read the notes and confirm that each important claim has visible support. If only the copy distinguishes the routes, revise the selection or label the missing visual evidence as a limit. Do not invent an image to hide that gap.

Aim for the PPTX and PDF to preserve the same image order, scale, crop, captions, and visual hierarchy. Inspect both rendered outputs when available. If a native PowerPoint renderer or viewer is unavailable, record what you checked, such as file structure or successful export, and leave visual parity unverified. Do not present structural inspection as a visual review.
