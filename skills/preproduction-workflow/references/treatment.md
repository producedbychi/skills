# Treatment mode

A treatment makes a creative choice that the client can approve and the crew can plan. It is not the strategic brief, a shot list, a script, or an image-generation prompt.

## Inputs and decision

Read the approved brief when one exists. If not, use [creative brief](brief.md) to extract a brief snapshot from the user's request and project material. State material assumptions. Do not require a separate brief file when the information is already clear.

Identify the audience promise, the one message that should survive the edit, the desired response, and the production limits. Review supplied visual references for the property they demonstrate, such as pacing, lighting, point of view, or tone. Do not copy a reference's distinctive composition, dialogue, or branding. Check that any product claim has a source and any required product or person can actually be shown.

Choose one lead direction unless the user requests alternatives or the decision calls for a comparison. Alternatives should differ in story structure, emotional approach, or production method, not just styling. Explain why the lead direction fits this audience, objective, channel, and available resources. If evidence is too thin to choose, ask the smallest set of questions that would settle the choice.

## Treatment output

Write a treatment at the level needed to approve a direction. Use these elements, combining headings when a small project does not need them separately:

1. **Working title and status.** Name the idea and mark it `proposed` or `approved` based on actual feedback.
2. **Creative thesis.** One or two sentences describing the central idea and the audience effect. Make the connection to the brief explicit.
3. **Story or experience.** Describe the opening, development, payoff, and call to action when relevant. A product film may use a demonstration sequence rather than a plot. Give the brand or product a clear role.
4. **Visual and sound direction.** Specify the framing, movement, color or lighting, pace, voice, music, sound, graphics, and performance choices that matter. Tie choices to the idea. Avoid camera jargon that does not change the result.
5. **Production approach.** Identify the necessary people, products, locations, assets, or generation work. Say which parts can come from supplied material and which need capture, licensing, or creation. Flag the expensive or uncertain choice.
6. **Deliverable fit.** Explain how the concept works in the requested durations, placements, and aspect ratios. Do not assume that a horizontal master will solve vertical cutdowns.
7. **References and decisions.** Name each useful reference and what it informs. List only decisions that affect approval or feasibility.

The treatment should give the breakdown a stable handoff: objective, audience promise, core message, story beats, visual and sound rules, deliverables, required elements, constraints, and unresolved decisions. If the user supplies a preferred concept, develop it rather than replacing it with a new one.

## Quality check

- Can someone explain the concept without reading the original prompt?
- Does each major beat support the brief rather than decorate it?
- Is the product, service, or subject recognizable and factually represented?
- Can the direction be made within known constraints, or are the unknowns visible?
- Are proposed choices and unverified claims clearly separate from approved facts?

Revise a weak treatment before handing it off. If only a crucial client decision can resolve the weakness, surface that decision instead of inventing certainty.
