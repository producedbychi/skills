# AV script mode

An AV script aligns exactly what the viewer sees with what they hear, beat by beat. It bridges an approved or proposed treatment and the production plan. Use it when the user asks for a video script or when `full` includes video. If the user supplies a script, preserve approved message and copy unless the requested revision changes them.

## Write the script

Read the brief and treatment when available. Identify the deliverable, target audience, intended runtime, placement, core message, required claims, call to action, and any approved voice or dialogue. If no treatment exists, write from the supplied concept and mark creative choices `proposed`. Ask about a missing decision only if it would change the message or structure. Otherwise draft with a visible assumption.

Header: deliverable and version, status, source direction, target runtime or `TBD`, audience/channel, and the decision or approval requested. Use a two-column table. Keep action, picture source or capture need, on-screen text, and graphics in **Video**; keep exact dialogue or voiceover, natural sound, music, and silence in **Audio**. Put a stable beat ID and estimated duration or time range at the start of each Video cell. If the runtime is unknown, use beat order and mark timing provisional rather than inventing a length. Do not mistake a storyboard or visual description for an AV script.

| Video | Audio |
| --- | --- |
| B01, 0:00-0:03. Action, framing, on-screen text, or graphic needed for the opening. | Exact approved or proposed spoken words; natural sound, music, or silence. |

Write spoken words as speakable copy, not a description of what the narrator might say. Distinguish exact dialogue from performer direction, and exact on-screen words from a graphics placeholder. Use sourced product facts and claims only; label unverified claims for review. If a factual claim appears in voiceover, screen text, or a demonstration, keep those expressions consistent. Show what the viewer understands without sound and what audio adds when sound is on. Note captions or accessibility requirements when the channel or brief calls for them.

For several video versions, write the main version and a version map. Give each cutdown a changed opening, retained message, payoff, and call to action; write a separate AV table when the edit or words differ materially. A crop-only version can be a production note. Email and landing-page copy are separate artifacts, not audio or video columns. Keep the script producible with known people, products, locations, and assets. Mark anything needing capture, licensing, generation, or client supply so the breakdown can plan it.

## Short-form gate

Approved facts are eligible to use, not a command to put every fact in every cut. Before writing a cutdown, separate facts that must appear in the video from details that can live in the caption or linked destination. Keep the main message, any qualification needed to avoid misleading viewers, and the call to action. Name the deferred details and where they belong.

For a cut of 15 seconds or less, default to at most four distinct large-text messages across the whole video, including the call to action. The end card should introduce no new requirement beyond the brand and one clear call to action. Avoid repeating every title in voiceover; accessibility captions are separate from large titles. With titles on screen, aim for about 25 spoken words or fewer in 15 seconds unless a timed read proves more is clear. If mandatory information cannot fit, flag the runtime conflict and propose a longer cut or a different layout. Do not deliver a crowded script as if a faster read solves it.

## Handoff and quality check

Read the spoken copy aloud or estimate it against the target duration, allowing time for action, pauses, titles, and the call to action. Check the combined load of speech and on-screen text, especially in short cutdowns. A cut can fit its voiceover and still leave too little time to read the titles or end card; remove or defer secondary detail instead of compressing the timecodes. If it runs long, cut or restructure it; do not merely shrink the timecodes. End with total estimated runtime, required assets or performances, version changes, and open approvals. Use stable beat IDs so the breakdown can trace each shot or asset back to the script.

- Does the opening establish a reason to keep watching within the intended format?
- Can a producer tell what appears and what is heard at each beat?
- Are spoken lines and screen words actual draft copy rather than placeholders disguised as finished text?
- Are spoken words, screen text, claims, and call to action consistent with the brief?
- Can the planned beats fit the runtime without rushing essential information?
- Does the script work within the supplied production limits, with unresolved needs visible?
