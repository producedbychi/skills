# Turning a service benefit into an image

When a service has few photographs, ask what visible change, choice, or task would make the approved benefit clear. An illustration can explain the service or customer's problem without claiming to document the advertiser's work. Keep the approved benefit distinct from evidence. A visual can promise what the service helps with, but only supplied proof can show what it has achieved.

The examples below are hypothetical teaching examples. Their offers, benefits, visuals, and brands are invented. They do not report campaign results or claim evidence. Use the reasoning, not the layouts.

## Creative service: a small video studio

Brief. "Short product films for independent food makers." Approved benefit: "show the detail that makes your product worth choosing." No client footage or staff portraits are available.

Concept. A camera names equipment. A near-photographic film still could pass as client work. Choose a paper-cut illustration of a loaf as layered shapes, with a bright incision splitting the crust into graphic planes and a strip of film perforations leading toward it. This translates the detail into a film idea, not a bakery ad.

Render direction. Use a tight horizontal crop. Let the cut and film-strip path lead, with open space for the supplied phrase "Short product films" and approved benefit. Use torn paper edges, visible paper grain, and two flat colors. The handmade medium signals illustration; the brief's own wording identifies the service.

Promise and evidence. The image suggests that a film can draw attention to product detail. It does not show the studio's work or prove that viewers will choose the product. A laptop with an editing timeline says "video production" and leaves the benefit unstated.

Brand fit. Keep the paper-cut construction and adapt paper color, cut shape, and type to verified brand assets. A playful studio can use irregular cuts; a spare identity can use clean geometry. Keep the service cue in the copy.

## Local service: home energy assessment

Brief. "Book a home energy assessment." Approved benefit: "find where drafts are getting in." No technician, customer home, or measured results are supplied.

Concept. A technician could look like proof of a real visit; a house icon says little about drafts. Choose an illustrated door cutaway. A blue ribbon of air bends under the threshold as a tissue lifts toward the gap. The small movement makes an invisible draft visible.

Render direction. Crop close to a doorway in a simplified, unidentifiable hallway. Set warm interior shapes against one narrow blue airflow ribbon; leave space for "Find where drafts get in." A drawn doorway can illustrate the situation without suggesting a customer inspection.

Promise and evidence. The image explains what the assessment looks for. It does not show an actual inspection or promise a measured saving. A smiling family signals comfort but leaves the service unclear.

Brand fit. Use the brand accent for the air ribbon and retain warm/cool contrast. Match the drawing style to the brand's existing illustration or icon system.

## Software and professional service: bookkeeping firm

Brief. "Monthly bookkeeping for solo consultants." Approved benefit: "know what is due before month-end." No interface, client records, or accountant portraits are available.

Concept. A fabricated dashboard could imply unapproved features; a calculator beside coffee is vague. Choose a paper calendar with blank task tabs near month-end and a folder marked "Invoices." It makes timing and organization visible without inventing an interface.

Render direction. Draw the calendar and folder from above. Let the approaching tabs lead to copy such as "Know what's due before month-end." Keep dates and tabs generic, with no completed marks or client details.

Promise and evidence. The calendar promises a clearer view of upcoming work. It does not prove a client avoided a missed payment. Generic charts risk looking like the firm's real product and suggesting features not in the brief.

Brand fit. Use verified colors and type. A digital-first brand can redraw the calendar as a crisp interface-like illustration, while keeping it distinct from a real screen. If the firm supplies an approved interface later, consider whether it explains the benefit better.
