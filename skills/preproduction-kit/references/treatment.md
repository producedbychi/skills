# Treatment mode

A treatment lets a client picture and choose the proposed experience before the script is written. It should make the idea compelling and testable against the brief, while giving the crew a plausible production approach. It is not a second brief, a shot list, a script, or an image-generation prompt.

## Inputs and decision

Read the approved brief when one exists. If not, use [creative brief](brief.md) to extract a brief snapshot from the user's request and project material. State material assumptions. Do not require a separate brief file when the information is already clear.

Identify the audience promise, the one message that should survive the edit, the desired response, and the production limits. Review supplied visual references for the property they demonstrate, such as pacing, lighting, point of view, or tone. Do not copy a reference's distinctive composition, dialogue, or branding. Check that any product claim has a source and any required product or person can actually be shown.

Choose one lead direction unless the user requests alternatives or the decision calls for a comparison. Alternatives should differ in story structure, emotional approach, or production method, not just styling. Explain why the lead direction fits this audience, objective, channel, and available resources. If essential brief decisions remain open, label the treatment a working proposal and show which choice could change it.

## Treatment output

Write a treatment at the level needed to approve a direction. Use these elements, combining headings when a small project does not need them separately:

1. **Working title and status.** Name the idea and mark it `proposed` or `approved` based on actual feedback.
2. **Creative thesis and reason.** One or two sentences describing the central idea, audience effect, and why this approach answers the brief. The idea should be distinctive enough to guide choices later.
3. **Story or experience.** In short present-tense prose, let the reader experience the opening, development, turning point or demonstration, payoff, and call to action when relevant. Show what the audience learns and feels at each step. Give the brand or product a clear role; do not replace the story with abstract adjectives.
4. **Visual and sound direction.** Specify the framing, movement, color or lighting, pace, voice, music, sound, graphics, and performance choices that materially shape the idea. Tie choices to the story. Use a few purposeful references or frames when available, naming what each illustrates, not implying it is approved or will be copied.
5. **Production approach.** Identify the necessary people, products, locations, assets, or generation work. Say which parts can come from supplied material and which need capture, licensing, or creation. Flag the expensive or uncertain choice.
6. **Deliverable fit.** Explain how the concept works in the requested durations, placements, and aspect ratios. For a campaign, show the job of each companion asset, such as what the email introduces and what the landing page must explain or enable. Do not imply that a horizontal master solves vertical cutdowns or that one piece of copy serves every channel.
7. **Approval question.** State the exact creative direction the client is being asked to choose, what is still provisional, and the few decisions that affect feasibility. Do not bury unresolved claims, access, or costs in the narrative.

The treatment should give the breakdown a stable handoff: objective, audience promise, core message, story beats, visual and sound rules, deliverables, required elements, constraints, and unresolved decisions. If the user supplies a preferred concept, develop it rather than replacing it with a new one.

## Quality check

- Can someone explain the concept and picture its key moments without reading the original prompt?
- Does each major beat support the brief rather than decorate it?
- Is the product, service, or subject recognizable and factually represented?
- Can the direction be made within known constraints, or are the unknowns visible?
- Are proposed choices and unverified claims clearly separate from approved facts?
- Is the requested approval about the concept, not accidentally an approval of price, rights, or final copy?

Revise a weak treatment before handing it off. If a missing client decision prevents a sound direction, name that decision instead of inventing certainty.
