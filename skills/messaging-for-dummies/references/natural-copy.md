# Natural copy

This reference adapts Unslop's editing principles for marketing copy. Use it within the existing drafting and interpretation check. It is a writing guide, not an AI detector or evidence of conversion performance.

## Make each sentence useful

Choose the strongest supported reason this buyer would care at this placement. Lead with the useful task, consequence, mechanism, or meaningful difference, whichever best answers the buyer's decision. Keep the product or service recognizable in the reader-visible copy. A category description can lead when identifying the offer is the reader's main need.

Use supplied facts as material to prioritize. In short ads and spoken pitches, include the detail needed to explain the main point rather than listing every capability. Technical explanations and offer terms need the precision and qualifications required for an informed decision. When a benefit is uncertain, lead with the supported capability.

Give each headline, supporting sentence, and action a distinct job. After the lead, add the mechanism, proof, decision-critical condition, or next step the reader still needs. Keep repetition when it resolves ambiguity or preserves accuracy. Replace generic claims about innovation or transformation with supplied specifics; keep missing proof visible in notes.

Research on concrete language in customer-service interactions supports attending to the customer's specific concern. Its findings are not a universal ad-conversion forecast. This skill applies specificity as an editorial principle. [Packard and Berger, Journal of Consumer Research](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/47/5/787/5873524).

## Choose words for their meaning

Use everyday words when they convey the same meaning as a longer alternative. Replace "utilize" with "use" and "in order to" with "to." Examine vague uses of "leverage," "empower," "seamless," and "elevate" for a more specific statement. Choose replacements because they clarify the message, not because a word appears on a blacklist.

Name the actor and action when known. Use passive wording when the actor is unknown or unimportant. Split sentences that require rereading. Keep needed articles, units, conditions, and relationships instead of compressing them into difficult fragments.

Use the same name for the same thing. Rotating through synonyms can make one service sound like several different offers. Keep useful technical terms, product names, and approved wording intact.

## Remove automatic writing patterns

Replace decorative phrases such as "fostering engagement" with the actual action or omit them. Examine repeated contrast formulas, forced groups of three, unsupported superlatives, and adjective lists. Keep a contrast or list when it explains a real difference or set of facts, rather than imposing a sentence pattern on the material.

State claims directly instead of using vague authority phrases such as "experts agree." An attribution should identify a real source already established in the assignment. Use qualifiers when they preserve accuracy, and remove strings of hedges that obscure the statement.

For publishable copy, remove assistant greetings, praise of the user's request, generic encouragement, and explanations of the drafting process. Follow the requested format for any separate editorial notes.

## Keep deliberate brand expression

Retain humor, distinctive vocabulary, imagery, and sentence rhythm that suit the approved voice and help communicate the idea. A metaphor can make an unfamiliar concept recognizable. A headline or CTA can be a fragment. Preserve such choices when their purpose is clear and their meaning remains accurate.

Use punctuation and emphasis to organize the message. Prefer periods and commas for separating thoughts. Use sentence-case headings and restrained formatting by default, while preserving approved campaign styling. Format for the actual placement rather than applying a prose-paragraph style to every headline, label, or image caption.

For a spoken pitch, read the sentence at a conversational pace. Simplify clause chains and stiff constructions while keeping the service and its useful purpose clear. Preserve distinctive phrasing that suits the voice and makes the point easier to remember.

## Memorable headlines

For headlines, consider a supported contrast or counterintuitive detail that makes the offer clearer and more memorable. Use direct wording when there is no meaningful contrast. Keep the offer identifiable and preserve its qualifications.

## Organize for the placement

Make the main point easy to find and keep supporting detail subordinate to it. For longer pages or emails, group related information, use descriptive headings when helpful, and place decision-critical facts where readers encounter the claim. A short ad can communicate through its headline and visual together. Choose the structure that helps readers find what they need, rather than adding sections to satisfy a formula.

The CDC's guidance supports message priority and an understandable action. Digital.gov addresses information hierarchy. Nielsen Norman Group's older web studies support concise, scannable, factual writing in their tested contexts, not guaranteed sales gains or a ban on expressive copy. [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/ccindex/tool/how-to-use.html), [Digital.gov](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/design), [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/).

## Finish in the existing check

Identify the exact word, sentence, or repeated pattern that sounds generic or obscures the offer. Make the focused edit, then check that the offer, evidence, conditions, and brand voice still match the assignment. Stop when those checks pass. Reading the reference does not require extra variants or a separate critique document.
