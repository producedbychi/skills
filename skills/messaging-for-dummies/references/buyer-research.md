# Buyer research and comprehension testing

Read this when the user requests a research-backed buyer profile, when developing positioning requires investigating buyer beliefs and behavior, or when the user asks to validate comprehension with real readers. A short rewrite can use existing context without this research workflow.

## Establish the evidence

Agree on the buyer segment and research question. Inspect available interviews, sales notes, reviews, support conversations, survey responses, or analytics relevant to that question. Search public sources when authorized and useful. Use the available tools and disclose inaccessible sources; a link alone is not inspected evidence. Arrange new interviews or distribute surveys only with the user's authorization.

Record a compact evidence log:

| Source and date | Exact statement or observed behavior | Context and segment | Interpretation | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Keep quotations separate from interpretation. Record the sample size and source bias. A complaint in a support ticket may reveal a real concern without showing how prevalent it is. A preference expressed in an interview does not establish purchasing behavior. A competitor's reviews reveal a possible concern to investigate, not a proven concern among this brand's customers.

Identify repeated themes, disagreements, missing information, and differences between segments. Explain confidence through the evidence available rather than presenting a score as scientific certainty. If evidence is sparse, deliver provisional hypotheses with the next question that would confirm or challenge each one.

## Translate findings into messaging

Connect each proposed message to a buyer situation, the evidence behind it, an actual offer capability, and relevant proof. Compare interpretations when the same wording may mean different things to different readers. Preserve a distinction between an audience aspiration and a promise the brand can fulfill.

For positioning or comparative messaging, identify alternatives buyers actually consider, including the current approach or postponing a change. Connect a supported capability or offer condition to the reason it matters in that comparison. Use interviews or sales evidence when available. With no verified alternative or advantage, label the comparison hypothetical and state the missing evidence. April Dunford's positioning guidance informs this approach; it is practitioner guidance, mainly for B2B, rather than proof of a universal conversion effect. [Dunford on differentiation](https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/the-no-differentiation-illusion).

Match claim evidence to the actual wording and its reasonable implications. A customer quote can document an experience without proving a typical quantified result. An approved creative line is not substantiation of a factual claim. The FTC's US advertising guidance covers both explicit and implied objective claims and calls for support before publication. Use this as a claim-check principle, not a completed legal review for every jurisdiction. [FTC advertising substantiation](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/ftc-policy-statement-regarding-advertising-substantiation).

For example, a buyer may say they value craftsmanship while repeatedly choosing the lowest quote. Explore what cost, deadlines, internal approval, or perceived risk explain the difference. The research should inform the offer and its explanation rather than judge the buyer's intelligence or character.

Deliver only the research outputs the user needs. A useful messaging brief can include the buyer situation, belief and behavior evidence, customer wording, decision uncertainty, proposed message, supporting proof, and unresolved question. It is not necessary to produce a fictional persona or a complete marketing plan.

## Validate understanding

Use representative intended readers and the actual artifact in a realistic context. Before showing it, agree on which facts the reader must understand and which false interpretations would matter. Separate information the artifact should communicate from details intentionally reserved for a later step.

Ask neutral questions after exposure:

- What do you think is being offered?
- Who do you think it is for?
- What would you expect to receive or happen?
- What does the claim mean to you, and what limits do you notice?
- What would you do next, and what questions remain?

Record their wording before explaining the offer or prompting them toward the intended answer. Compare responses with the agreed facts and material misunderstandings. Include divergent answers instead of averaging them away. Report the method, sample, observed misunderstandings, and changes made. A small qualitative sample can expose problems; it does not establish market-wide comprehension or a conversion lift.

Ask readers to explain the message in their own words rather than merely confirm that it is clear. Digital.gov describes this as paraphrase testing. After revision, test the changed artifact when participants and authorization are available. [Digital.gov paraphrase testing](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/test/paraphrase-testing).

When the assignment also asks about message appeal or positioning, follow the comprehension questions with neutral prompts:

- How does this relate to a decision or task you face, if at all?
- Which parts are useful or unimportant to you, and why?
- What would you compare this with? What differences do you notice?
- What makes you believe or doubt the claim?

Keep evidence of clarity, relevance, value, differentiation, and hesitation separate. If readers misidentify the offer, fix that before interpreting their preference feedback. If they understand it but have little use for it, report a relevance or offer problem. Wynter's B2B message-testing framework informs this diagnostic distinction; its practitioner framework is not evidence that every message must use the same sequence or layout. [Wynter message layers](https://wynter.com/post/b2b-message-layers-framework-wynter).

If testing with actual readers is unavailable, deliver a test plan. An agent's simulated reader review is an editorial aid, not human research.

## Evaluate conversion separately

Comprehension, stated preference, clicks, qualified inquiries, and purchases are different outcomes. Choose the outcome that matches the business goal. A clear message can still describe an unwanted offer. A more engaging message can attract the wrong buyers.

When performance evaluation is authorized, compare messages under reasonably comparable conditions and disclose confounding changes. Report whether the evidence shows understanding, engagement, or actual conversion. Use the result to revise the relevant message or offer explanation rather than add an untested universal rule.
