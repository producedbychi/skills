# Brand edge

Develop a distinctive strategy someone can use to make decisions. Explain who the brand serves, what they want, why this offer fits, and what the business must deliver to support its promise. Find a customer-relevant position the brand can credibly occupy against the alternatives this audience actually considers. A clear category description is a starting point, not evidence of differentiation. Formulate the core messaging from the strategic choice. Apply this to products, services, software, local businesses, creators, and organizations whose audience or payer differs from the end user.

The result is a strategic brief and messaging foundation. Adapt its depth to the assignment. A campaign direction is not automatically a permanent niche for the whole company. Naming, identity design, a complete marketing plan, and rendered ads are separate deliverables unless requested.

## 1. Establish the decision

Read the request, relevant conversation, supplied materials, and available brand or product-marketing context. Reuse approved positioning and constraints. Earlier AI drafts are proposals, not proof of capabilities or customer demand.

Identify what needs deciding, such as a new brand's audience, an existing offer's differentiation, or a campaign's lead promise. Establish the scope, business objective, actual offer, and what must stay unchanged. For an established brand, distinguish a messaging problem from a positioning or product problem before recommending change.

Ask a focused question when a missing answer would change the audience, business scope, or material promise. Use the host's native question interface when available. Gather only unanswered information. When the user requests a quick concept or best guess, proceed with labeled assumptions rather than a compulsory workshop. If the business or offer itself is unknown, ask what it sells before choosing a strategy.

Complete this step when the strategic decision and its boundaries are clear, or explicitly provisional.

## 2. Connect the buyer situation to the actual offer

Establish the desired result, buying trigger, alternatives considered, decision criteria, and objections from available evidence. Include aspirations, enjoyment, belonging, or convenience when relevant. A strategy does not need to manufacture fear or a painful problem. Separate users, buyers, approvers, and beneficiaries when their priorities differ.

Record the offer's actual capabilities, experience, delivery constraints, and proof. Connect each proposed advantage to a buyer-relevant consequence and the alternative it improves on. Include the customer's current method or doing nothing when relevant. Differentiate against the real comparison, not a caricature of competitors. A supported combination of attributes may matter without being unique worldwide.

Look for an opportunity in the mismatch between what customers want and what the alternatives prioritize. Examine workarounds, overlooked uses, conflicting preferences, overlooked desires, or a buying context the category handles poorly. Pair that insight with an existing strength or feasible proposed change. Describe the insight in the customer's situation before turning it into a slogan. Treat apparent opportunities as hypotheses until their evidence supports the conclusion.

Use these evidence labels in the working notes and for consequential claims in the brief:

- Observed means an inspected source directly records the fact, statement, or behavior. Identify the source and its context.
- Supplied means the user or brand reports the fact or audience belief. Identify who supplied it.
- Hypothetical means an inference, proposed direction, or untested assumption.

A customer quote establishes that person's view. Broader conclusions need relevant supporting evidence. Competitor reviews and public discussions are proxies for this brand's buyers. An approved creative direction does not substantiate results, exclusivity, or testimonials within it.

Read [buyer evidence](references/buyer-evidence.md) when gathering missing customer or competitor evidence, evaluating a claimed market gap, or designing a validation step. Research only the uncertainty that matters to this decision. Use supplied evidence when sufficient. With no browsing access or when the user requests no research, identify unresolved comparisons rather than asserting an empty market.

Complete this step when the relevant buyer situation, actual offer, and alternatives are described, with support and uncertainty distinguishable.

## 3. Choose the positioning

For new positioning or a request for a more distinctive direction, read [distinctive positioning](references/distinctive-positioning.md). Use its opportunity questions and worked comparisons to develop a small set of materially different strategic options. Vary the customer situation, value, experience, or way of reaching the buyer where the assignment permits. Each option needs an insight, a reason to choose this brand, the business choice that makes it credible, and its evidence status. A different slogan, narrower demographic label, or invented category name alone is not another strategy.

Compare the options for buyer relevance, distinctiveness against actual alternatives, delivery feasibility, supporting evidence, ability to reach the audience, and commercial fit. Identify the main weakness and tradeoff of each credible candidate. Recommend the strongest coherent choice, explaining what makes it less interchangeable with alternatives and why the other plausible options were not selected. Explain tradeoffs without invented precision or automatic numeric scores. Keep the exploration internal unless presenting alternatives would help the user decide.

Respect approved audience choices. Within them, focus on a recognizable need or buying situation. A new campaign's focus need not exclude other customers from the business. When evidence cannot distinguish options, recommend a provisional direction and name the observation that would change the choice.

State the chosen audience and situation, the product or service category buyers can understand, the main intended value, and the supported reason to choose it over the relevant alternative. Explain the business choice, capability, experience, or customer relationship that produces that value. Price, convenience, craft, identity, or specialization can lead when the evidence supports their importance. A position may be distinctive through a meaningful combination rather than an exclusive feature. Keep existing capabilities separate from proposed improvements.

Run the interchangeability check on the strategic claim before writing polished copy. If a relevant alternative could truthfully use the same claim with only its name changed, identify the overlap and look for a more specific insight or credible commitment. Check a claimed combination against the closest credible alternative as a whole. If that alternative already offers the same combination, describe it as shared value, not an established competitive difference. Explain what the brand would have to prioritize, change, or demonstrate to make the position real, and the cost or tradeoff of that choice.

If the actual offer cannot support a distinctive position, state that differentiation remains unresolved. Supply the best supported current position. When offer changes are permitted, propose a concrete direction with its dependencies. When the offer must stay fixed, explore a relevant buying situation, desired experience, or brand expression it can already support. Present this as a positioning hypothesis, not a proven market gap or competitive advantage. Recommend improvements within the assignment, with approval and delivery still required. Clever wording cannot make an unbuilt capability real. Category creation requires a reason and an explanation of the cost of teaching customers a new category.

Complete this step when the recommendation identifies the customer insight, a meaningful difference against relevant alternatives, the choice that supports it, and its tradeoffs. If a required part is missing, mark the position provisional or undifferentiated and identify what would resolve it. Reserve exclusivity claims for evidence sufficient to support their scope.

## 4. Formulate the messaging foundation

Write an internal positioning statement that captures the choice. Use natural wording rather than a mandatory fill-in formula. Then draft a buyer-facing value proposition that makes the actual offer recognizable and leads with the strongest supported reason it matters. Carry the decision-bearing reason into the core message rather than leaving it only in the rationale. A short headline and explanatory line can work together; neither needs to carry every detail alone.

Add supporting messages only when they supply distinct reasons or evidence. For each material promise, identify the current proof, conditions, or proof still needed. Trace the mechanism and fulfillment details in the message to actual capabilities. A buyer's need does not establish that the offer provides the corresponding feature or service. Address the objections that could block this buyer's decision. Set the desired next action and distinguish existing follow-through from recommendations.

Use the supplied voice and sourced customer language. Prefer specific descriptions over unsupported superlatives and abstract slogans. Technical detail belongs when it explains an advantage the audience cares about. Preserve material qualifications. Frame proposed outcomes as intentions unless evidence supports a stronger claim. Label prototypes as illustrative copy, and keep unsupported claims out of publishable copy. Place a proposed-offer label immediately before copy that relies on unadopted delivery changes. Provide a current-offer version when the lead must describe what exists. When differentiation is unresolved, still write useful copy around a relevant use, desire, or occasion. Explain the hypothesis status in the brief. Customer-facing copy may invite a use the actual offer supports without claiming a guaranteed benefit or established customer behavior. That invitation is not proof of competitive difference.

Complete this step when the core message expresses the chosen strategy, the reader can identify the offer, and supporting claims have the required proof or qualifications.

## 5. Apply the choice where it matters

For a full brand strategy, connect the recommendation to the relevant business and creative decisions. For a narrow positioning assignment, include only the implications needed to act on that choice.

Read [brand application](references/brand-application.md) when translating strategy into offer design, brand expression, partnerships, distribution, or extensions. For each recommendation, state the buyer need it serves, what to keep or change, and the dependency or uncertainty. Prioritize the changes needed to make the promise true before cosmetic changes.

Use approved names and brand assets unless the assignment includes revising them. Distinguish creative suggestions from finished designs and prospective partners from actual agreements. Preserve the audience's dignity. Shared identity can support relevance without treating that identity as a defect.

Complete this step when the relevant recommendations follow from the chosen strategy and each can be understood as a concrete decision, rather than a list of fashionable tactics.

## 6. Check and deliver

Before delivery, check the recommendation against the sources and assignment:

- Does the audience have a recognizable situation or desire, rather than only demographic details?
- Does the promise follow from what the business can deliver, with proposed improvements identified?
- Is the reason to choose it meaningful against the actual alternatives, with unsupported comparisons removed?
- Is the recommendation distinctive beyond its wording, and is any unresolved differentiation disclosed?
- Do the core message and applied decisions support the same choice?
- Have conflicting buyer priorities, operational limits, and consequential unknowns remained visible?
- Does the recommendation preserve approved brand choices and the requested scope?

Repair specific inconsistencies. Deliver the recommendation first, followed by the messaging foundation, relevant applications, and the uncertainty most likely to change the strategy. Suggest the smallest useful next learning step and what finding would support or overturn the recommendation. Keep detailed working tables internal unless they help the user decide.

For a full assignment, produce a reusable brief with the chosen audience and buying situation, customer insight, positioning and rationale, relevant alternatives, the source of distinctiveness and its tradeoffs, core message and support, relevant applications, evidence status, and next decision. State whether the distinctiveness exists today, depends on proposed changes, or remains unresolved. For a narrow assignment, return that requested artifact instead of the full brief. Use the requested file format or an available document tool for client-facing documents. If only text is available, return the content and state the export limit.

When saving a brief, use the user's chosen location or `brand-strategy.md` in the requested project. On revisions, state what changed and why. Preserve existing shared context unless updating it is part of the request. If the user asks to integrate with Corey's skills, map the approved conclusions into the existing product-marketing document without silently replacing other sections or creating a competing source of truth.

This skill works alone. When further execution is requested, the brief can inform messaging-for-dummies, ad-image-generator, or other available skills. Pass along the approved choices, evidence boundaries, exact copy if approved, and real assets. A handoff does not mean those skills have run. Editorial review checks consistency, not customer demand or conversion performance.
