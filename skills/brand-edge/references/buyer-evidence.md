# Buyer evidence

Read this when a strategic choice needs customer or competitive research, a claimed gap needs checking, or the user needs a validation plan. Use the evidence labels defined in `SKILL.md`.

## Choose the question before gathering sources

State which uncertainty could change the recommendation. Examples include whether buyers care about a proposed benefit, what they use instead, why they postpone a purchase, or whether competitors already solve the supposed gap. Distinguish uncertainty about demand from uncertainty about delivery or the economics of serving the audience.

Reuse supplied interviews, sales notes, reviews, support records, performance data, and offer documentation. For new businesses, adjacent or competitor evidence can guide a hypothesis while first-party evidence is unavailable. A lack of interviews is not a reason to fabricate a persona or refuse a provisional concept.

For an existing business, compare successful customers with losses, dissatisfied customers, and people who stayed with their current method when those records exist. Identify who each source represents. Separate what people say they prefer from what they actually selected, paid for, or abandoned.

## Extract what changes a decision

For each useful observation, retain the source, date when available, customer situation, exact statement or behavior, and strategic implication. Quote only text actually present in the source. Label paraphrases as paraphrases. Group evidence by relevant buying situation before drawing conclusions. Reposts of one statement count as one source.

Look for the event that prompted action, the result sought, the alternatives tried, the decision criterion that mattered, and the obstacle to switching. Positive motivation can be as relevant as frustration. A testimonial about quality does not automatically support faster sales or increased revenue.

Track contradictions. Buyers who praise flexibility may choose a narrower offer because its scope is easier to evaluate. That observation can change positioning, but its explanation remains a hypothesis until supported. Weight evidence by relevance and quality, not by a universal source-count threshold. Describe coverage and limitations instead of treating a small sample as representative of a market.

## Check a market gap

Search the need, category, and audience, including different words customers use for them. Inspect direct competitors and substitute solutions. Use current product pages for capabilities and terms, and customer accounts for their experience. A competitor's headline establishes what it claims, not whether it delivers or converts.

Report what the inspected alternatives cover and what remains uncertain. A niche product already existing can still leave an opportunity in experience, access, assortment, or another customer-relevant difference. Conversely, an empty search result may reflect different terminology, poor demand, or a difficult business model.

For a proposed differentiated position, identify which part alternatives already offer and which part is a meaningful difference. Check product capabilities and customer experience as well as slogans. A missing phrase on competitors' homepages does not establish a missing capability. Look for evidence that customers care about the proposed difference, and search for an alternative that contradicts the opportunity hypothesis. If one exists, refine the position or explain the remaining advantage. Keep the search scope visible when describing originality.

Bound negative findings to the sources inspected. Claims such as "nobody serves this audience," "the only," or "the fastest" require evidence sufficient for that scope. Keep proposed features and brand names separate from current market facts. Naming exploration does not establish trademark availability.

Stop the research pass when it can support a provisional choice with named uncertainties, or when the next useful evidence requires direct customer access. Do not keep collecting examples that repeat the same observation. If sources or tools are inaccessible, report that limit and use an explicitly hypothetical direction.

## Choose a learning step that can change the recommendation

Match the method to the unresolved question:

| Question | Useful check | What it establishes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do people recognize the offer and its use? | Show a short message to relevant unfamiliar people and ask them to explain it unaided. | Comprehension in that sample, not willingness to buy. |
| Does the need motivate action? | Ask about a recent real purchase or attempt to solve the problem, including what they spent or delayed. | Evidence about behavior and priorities, not a hypothetical promise to purchase. |
| Can the business deliver the proposed advantage? | Review a prototype or service pilot against the promised experience and operating constraints. | Delivery feasibility, not demand by itself. |
| Is the reason to choose credible? | Ask buyers to compare the offer with their actual current alternative and explain the tradeoffs. | Decision relevance and objections in that sample. |
| Does the strategy improve business results? | Test the relevant offer or message with a defined audience and meaningful conversion or sales outcome. | Performance in the tested conditions, with traffic quality and measurement limits considered. |

State the observation that would change the recommendation before the check. Polite agreement, an attractive mockup, and an agent's self-review are insufficient evidence of demand. Recommend outreach, prototypes, or live tests when useful. Carry them out only when the user authorizes that work.
