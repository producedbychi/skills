# Distinctive positioning

Read this when developing new positioning or strengthening an interchangeable direction. The goal is a meaningful, credible difference in the audience's actual decision. Novel wording and worldwide exclusivity are different from that goal.

## Find the opportunity before naming the position

Explore questions that fit the business and its evidence. Use these as ways to think, not required output sections:

- What do customers adapt, combine, tolerate, or pay extra for because existing options miss something?
- Which situation changes what matters, even among people in the same demographic or industry?
- Where does the category optimize a feature or experience that this audience would trade away for something else?
- What do satisfied customers value that the brand treats as incidental?
- What aspiration or identity do customers express that the usual category presentation overlooks?
- Which existing capability, access, experience, relationship, or proof asset would be difficult for the actual alternative to match?
- Could a different place, partner, purchase moment, or delivery model make the same offer more useful?

Write the potential insight as a situation and consequence. "Managers want high-quality video" is a broad preference. "These managers struggle to explain a product's operation with the materials they currently have" identifies a more specific problem, but still needs evidence. Then determine what the brand could do differently for that situation.

With sparse context, offer creative hypotheses and show what they depend on. With existing research, preserve the distinction between the observation and its interpretation. Treat a founder's original idea as a candidate, not the inevitable answer.

## Compare strategic choices

Build candidates around different reasons to choose, rather than multiple headlines for the same benefit. The alternatives may serve different buying situations, emphasize different strengths, or propose different offer changes. Keep approved constraints intact.

For each credible candidate, establish:

| Decision | What the comparison needs |
| --- | --- |
| Customer insight | The situation or desire, supporting evidence, and why it matters to the buying decision. |
| Relevant alternative | What this customer actually uses or considers, and what it already does well. |
| Difference | The capability, experience, relationship, or combination that makes this offer preferable for this situation. |
| Commitment | What the business must prioritize, change, retain, or demonstrate to make that difference real. |
| Tradeoff | The audience, flexibility, cost, capacity, or other advantage the business gives up. |
| Credibility | What exists now, what is proposed, and what could overturn the recommendation. |

Use a table when it makes competing options easier to judge. Otherwise keep it in the working notes. Relevance and feasibility constrain novelty. A surprising idea that buyers do not care about, cannot recognize, or the business cannot deliver is a weak position.

Distinctiveness can be relative to the customer's comparison set. A combination of specialization, recognizable experience, and supporting proof can differentiate a brand without an exclusive invention. A repeatable identity or audience relationship may contribute, but explain the reason it matters and what supports it. A competitor's ability to copy a claim tomorrow differs from its ability to deliver the experience today. Assess both rather than claiming permanent protection.

When every option is interchangeable, investigate the missing insight or propose a substantive offer change. Do not invent uniqueness to satisfy the assignment. Keep the strongest supported current position and the proposed differentiated concept separate.

## Worked comparisons

These fictional examples demonstrate decisions. They are not market findings, validated opportunities, copy templates, or recommendations for the user's business.

### A film studio

Assume a studio makes product films, and supplied interviews describe buyers whose sales teams reuse the films in demonstrations. The studio has product-operation samples, but has not shown sales impact.

"Product films that explain your product" describes the category. "Cinematic films for innovative brands" adds tone but still gives little reason to choose the studio.

One candidate leads with visual craft for brand launches. Another addresses the need for sales teams to show specific operations during a conversation. The second may support a proposed demonstration-film service with modular chapters organized around real buyer questions. Its business commitment would be to learn the product's use and plan the chapters with sales users. The tradeoff could be more discovery work and less freedom to organize the film solely as a visual narrative.

Select that direction only if the supplied need is relevant and the studio can support the service. Modular delivery is proposed unless it exists. Review sample films with intended sales users to assess usefulness. Avoid promising more sales. If evidence instead favors launch awareness and the studio's distinctive craft, choose that direction rather than forcing a functional-demo niche.

### A consumer product

Assume customer research describes people who protect exposed scalps but dislike visible residue. Existing products already address scalp use. A proposed finish and shade-selection system is untested.

"Sunscreen for bald people" gives the audience and category. The opportunity hypothesis is a preferred appearance during wear, rather than an unserved demographic. Compare formula or finish improvements with easier selection and use. Packaging could support the latter, but a head-shaped cap alone does not address the observed problem.

A candidate position would require a product experience customers prefer to the inspected alternatives. Its dependencies include formulation feasibility, appropriate professional review, testing, and viable production. If these cannot support the proposed difference, retain the concept as a hypothesis or choose another opportunity. A name, tagline, and influencer partnership can express a strategy, but cannot establish its product advantage.

### A ceramics brand

Assume the brand sells handmade tableware. Relevant supplied customer accounts describe replacing individual broken pieces, and the brand already supports replacement orders for continuing collections. Other inspected alternatives sell sets without that replacement option.

"Beautiful handmade ceramics" is accurate but interchangeable. A replacement-supported collection offers a more specific reason to choose for buyers who want to build and maintain a table setting over time. Its position could emphasize collecting usable pieces gradually, supported by actual replacement access.

Compare that direction with seasonal collectible designs. The replacement direction depends on continuity and manageable inventory. Seasonal turnover may create more novelty but undermine that experience. Select based on the audience and business constraints. Qualify availability using real terms, and avoid lifetime or perpetual replacement promises. The difference is the offer's continuity, not a claim that no ceramics brand anywhere provides replacements.

## Deliver the insight with the recommendation

Explain why this position is more useful or meaningful for the chosen buyer than the ordinary category claim. Identify what makes it distinctive now, or the proposed change required. Include the strongest uncertainty and the finding that would change the choice.

Keep the category recognizable in customer-facing messaging. A distinctive position does not require a cryptic slogan, a new category, or an exaggerated comparison. The strategy should remain understandable after removing decorative language.
